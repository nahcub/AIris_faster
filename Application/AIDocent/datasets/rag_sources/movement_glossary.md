# Northern Renaissance: Period, Schools, and Technique Glossary

## Northern Renaissance: Period Overview (1450-1550)

The Northern Renaissance refers to the flowering of art north of the Alps — chiefly
in the Holy Roman Empire (present-day Germany), Flanders, and the Low Countries —
roughly contemporary with the Italian Renaissance but developing along its own path.
Where Italian painters pursued classical antiquity and idealized form, Northern
painters built their innovations on close observation of the physical world: fabric,
skin, light on metal, the grain of wood. Two forces shaped the century: Johannes
Gutenberg's movable-type printing press (c. 1450), which made books and printed
images affordable and let religious and humanist ideas circulate fast, and the rise
of wealthy merchant and burgher classes in trading cities (Bruges, Antwerp,
Nuremberg, Augsburg) who commissioned portraits and devotional panels once reserved
for royalty and the Church. By the early 1500s, growing contact with Italy — through
trade, travel, and prints — began to blend classical Italian forms into what had been
a distinctly Northern visual language, especially in the Dutch school (see below).
This is also the century of the Protestant Reformation (Luther's 95 Theses, 1517),
which reshaped what German patrons commissioned and, for painters like Lucas
Cranach the Elder, who they worked for.

## German School

German painting of this period is closely tied to the graphic arts: woodcut and
engraving, which German printmakers — above all Albrecht Dürer — brought to a level
of technical and artistic sophistication not seen before. Dürer's woodcut series
such as the *Apocalypse* (1498) showed that a cheap, reproducible medium could carry
the same ambition as panel painting, and his prints circulated his style across
Europe faster than any single painting could. German painting favored dense,
expressive detail, sharp outline, and often an intensity of emotion or gesture —
visible in Matthias Grünewald's raw depictions of the Crucifixion — alongside a
strong portrait tradition (Hans Holbein the Younger, Bernhard Strigel). The
Reformation split German patronage: some painters, like Cranach the Elder, became
closely associated with the new Protestant movement (he was a friend of Martin
Luther and painted his portrait repeatedly), while others continued serving
Catholic patrons and workshops producing altarpieces for churches that had not
broken from Rome.

## Flemish School (Early Netherlandish)

The Flemish school — also called Early Netherlandish painting — centers on the
cities of Bruges, Ghent, and later Antwerp. Its founding generation, Jan van Eyck
and Robert Campin in the early 1400s, pioneered a level of illusionistic detail in
oil paint that made surfaces — velvet, fur, polished brass, glass — look almost
tangible; Rogier van der Weyden extended this into intensely emotional religious
narrative. The preferred subject matter stayed overwhelmingly religious — Madonnas,
Crucifixions, altarpieces — usually painted on wood panel, often as diptychs
(two hinged panels) or triptychs/polyptychs (three or more). Flemish workshops
operated on something close to an assembly-line model: a master would design and
paint the most important passages (faces, hands) while trained assistants completed
drapery, backgrounds, and secondary figures, which let workshops like those of Hans
Memling or Gerard David produce high-quality panels in volume for an export market
that reached across Europe. Later Flemish painters (Joos van Cleve, Quentin Massys,
Jan Gossart) increasingly absorbed Italian Renaissance motifs — classical
architecture, contrapposto poses — without abandoning the Flemish commitment to
minute surface detail.

## Dutch School and "Romanism"

The Dutch school, centered on cities like Utrecht and Haarlem, developed somewhat
later and more directly under Italian influence than its Flemish neighbor. Painters
such as Jan van Scorel and Maerten van Heemskerck traveled to Rome, studied
antique sculpture and the work of Michelangelo and Raphael firsthand, and brought
back a style — sometimes called "Romanism" — that combined classical anatomy,
monumental composition, and architectural settings with the Northern habit of
dense, detailed religious narrative. Scorel in particular is credited with
introducing Italianate composition to Dutch painting after his travels, and later
worked as a canon and de facto leader of the painters' guild in Utrecht, giving him
outsized influence on the generation that followed, including Heemskerck.

## Technique Glossary

- **Oil on panel (oil on wood)** — the standard support and medium for Northern
  paintings of this period, predating the later shift to canvas. Oak was the
  preferred wood in the Low Countries; panels were prepared with a chalk-and-glue
  ground (gesso) before painting began.
- **Underdrawing** — a preparatory drawing made directly on the primed panel,
  usually in a thin black liquid or dry medium, sketching out composition and
  contours before paint was applied. Early Netherlandish painters (following Jan
  van Eyck's workshop practice) sometimes used pricking-and-pouncing to transfer a
  design precisely from a separate drawing onto the panel.
- **Glazing** — building up color through thin, translucent layers of oil paint over
  an underlying layer, exploiting oil's slow drying time and transparency to create
  depth, luminosity, and lifelike rendering of materials like skin, fabric, and
  glass. Considered one of the technical breakthroughs of the Early Netherlandish
  school.
- **Diptych / Triptych / Polyptych** — panel paintings hinged together in two,
  three, or more sections respectively, often used as portable or opening
  altarpieces; the outer wings were frequently painted with grisaille (grey
  monochrome) images visible when the work was closed.
- **Altarpiece** — a panel or ensemble of panels placed behind or above a church
  altar, typically depicting devotional or narrative religious scenes tied to the
  saint or feast the altar was dedicated to.
- **Iconography** — the study of the symbolic content of images: which attributes
  identify which saint (a key for St. Peter, a wheel for St. Catherine), which
  objects in a domestic scene carry religious symbolism (a hidden crucifix, a
  particular flower), and so on. Early Netherlandish painting is especially known
  for "disguised symbolism," where ordinary household objects double as religious
  symbols within an otherwise naturalistic scene.
- **Workshop / studio production** — the common practice of a master painter running
  a workshop with assistants and apprentices who executed parts of a commission
  under the master's design and supervision; this explains why quality and
  attribution can vary within a single painter's body of work, and why some
  paintings are catalogued as "workshop of" or "circle of" a given master.
